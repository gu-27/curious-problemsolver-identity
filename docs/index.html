Conceptual & Research Foundation

The Curious Problem Solver

A Process-Based Identity Framework for Sustainable Growth in Changing IT Environments

John Correia · Founder, Inland NW AI · Professor of Information Systems, Gonzaga University

Most technology issues are not technology problems at all. They persist because the people inside the organization haven't changed how they think about problems, or about themselves. This framework addresses that gap: what it means to build a curious problem solver identity, why it sustains change where training alone cannot, and what the research says about how to develop it.

Section 01
The Foundation

Why Identity, Not Skills

Most organizations invest in skill-building: master this tool, achieve this metric, complete this certification. But research in behavior change consistently shows that sustainable transformation begins not with skills, but with identity.

From James Clear's work synthesizing identity-based habit formation: your current behaviors reflect your current identity. When you shift from "I need to learn AI" to "I am a curious problem solver with technology," your behaviors follow naturally and durably. This is not motivational language. It is supported by neuroscience and behavioral psychology. Repeated identity-aligned actions rewire the brain for lasting change.

Michelle Carter's research on IT Identity provides the empirical foundation specific to technology adoption: IT identity, defined as the extent to which an individual views use of technology as integral to their sense of self, predicts both exploratory and feature-based technology usage in ways that existing competency or skill models do not. Identity precedes behavior. Identity sustains behavior where training alone cannot.

Process-Based vs. Outcome-Based Identity

A process-based identity is habitual and automatic in more diverse situations. "I am a curious problem solver with technology" means curiosity and problem definition surface as a default response across contexts, not as techniques you remember to apply only when the pressure is high.

An outcome-based identity ("I am an AI expert," "I am successful") is fragile. When the outcome shifts, and in today's AI landscape it will, the identity fractures. A process-based identity ("I am someone who asks questions and solves problems rigorously") is resilient. It survives failure, adaptation, and transformation because the process, not the outcome, is who you are.

This distinction matters significantly for organizations navigating AI adoption. Employees and leaders who anchor their identities to mastery of specific tools resist change when those tools evolve. Those who anchor to the process of curious, rigorous problem-solving adapt, lead others through uncertainty, and create sustained value.

Where This Framework Came From

This framework did not begin as a theory. It grew from a recurring pattern observed across years of teaching information systems to students preparing to enter a technology-driven workforce.

Students reveal their identities early. Some, when confronted with an unfamiliar system or an open-ended problem, say things like "I am just not a technology person" or "I'm not good at this kind of thing." Others say "I love figuring things out" or "I don't know the answer yet, but I want to find it." These are not statements about skill. They are statements about identity, and they predict behavior with striking consistency. The student who says "I'm not a technology person" approaches every new tool as a confirmation of a belief already formed. The student who says "I love figuring things out" approaches the same tool as a problem worth exploring.

What both the research and the classroom confirm is that the identity comes first. The skill follows. Intentional teaching focused on building a curious problem solver identity serves two groups at once: it strengthens and extends the students who already carry strong problem-solving and technology identities, giving their instincts a framework and language; and it provides a genuine bridge for those who have told themselves they are not technology people, opening a path into engagement that skill-focused instruction alone rarely creates.

The Curious Problem Solver framework is an attempt to name that orientation, ground it in research, and make it teachable.

Section 02
The Structure

The Two Dimensions: A Yin-Yang Structure

The Curious Problem Solver identity has two essential dimensions. They are not sequential. They are not hierarchical. Each is incomplete without the other: curiosity without problem-solving can become a waste, and problem-solving without curiosity can become too rigid.

Dimension 1

Curiosity

An objective stance toward new things, staying open and exploratory rather than reactive or judgmental.

Dimension 2

Problem Solving

Returning to the problem assures that exploration ultimately adds value to the bottom line.

Dimension 1: Curiosity and Objectivity

Curiosity, in this framework, draws on three converging lines of research: Western social psychology, contemporary neuroscience, and Eastern contemplative traditions.

Ellen Langer's foundational research at Harvard represents what is now recognized as Western mindfulness: a cognitive and behavioral account of how people engage with their external environment. Mindlessness, in Langer's framework, is not inattention; it is rigid adherence to fixed mental categories formed in the past. People operating mindlessly apply old rules to new contexts, fail to notice what has changed, and respond to novelty with the patterns they already know. Mindfulness, by contrast, is active noticing: drawing new distinctions, remaining sensitive to context, staying alert to what is actually present rather than what is expected. This is the cognitive foundation of curiosity, and it maps directly onto the challenge organizations face in AI adoption, where the tendency to apply familiar frameworks to genuinely new situations is the primary source of misapplication and missed opportunity.

Judson Brewer's research represents a different but complementary tradition: Eastern mindfulness, which focuses on internal experience rather than external environment. Where Langer asks you to notice what is happening around you with fresh eyes, Brewer's framework asks you to notice what is happening inside you, including your emotions, thoughts, and physical sensations, with the same objectivity. When you approach a setback or an unfamiliar situation with judgment ("I shouldn't feel this way," "This threatens my expertise"), you activate reactivity and avoidance. Curiosity interrupts that cycle by turning attention toward internal experience rather than away from it. This shift from evaluation to observation disengages anxiety pathways and opens more adaptive thinking.

Together, Langer's Western and Brewer's Eastern traditions converge on the same practical capacity: the ability to observe both your external context and your internal reactions with enough objectivity to respond thoughtfully rather than reactively. In organizational contexts, this is what allows a leader to say "I don't fully understand this yet" without shame, and a team to ask "What went wrong?" rather than "Who failed?"

Research also shows that curiosity correlates strongly with growth mindset, openness to feedback, reduced confirmation bias, and higher overall well-being, making it not just a professional asset but a human one.

Dimension 2: Grounding Curiosity in Problem Solving

Curiosity without a problem to anchor on generates interesting observations that never land. Problem solving is what makes curiosity valuable: it gives exploration a destination and ensures that what you learn creates value for someone. The problem is not just a starting point; it is a frame you return to throughout. Using a problem as a framing device, either as a starting point that directs exploration or as a landing point that open curiosity can return to, is a powerful complement to the curious perspective.

The discipline embedded in this dimension is first-principles thinking: not a rigid procedure, but a habit of mind that consistently asks what is actually true here, stripped of assumption. As James Clear describes it, first principles thinking means identifying your current assumptions, breaking them down to their foundational elements, and rebuilding your reasoning from there, rather than reasoning by analogy from what already exists or what convention assumes to be true. Most constraints that feel fixed are inherited assumptions. When you strip them away, the solution space opens.

Applied to problem solving, first principles ask: What is the actual problem? Not the symptom, not the assumed solution, but the problem itself. Can you state it simply enough to evaluate whether you have solved it? This discipline forces precision and prevents the common failure mode of building solutions to problems that were never clearly defined.

When curiosity and problem solving work together, several things follow:

Exploration becomes grounded
You follow curiosity into new ideas and capabilities, but always return to whether this addresses a real, defined problem.
Waste decreases
Not every interesting possibility gets pursued. Only those aligned to a verified need move forward.
Value becomes communicable
You can explain not just what you built, but why it matters and how you know it worked.
Learning compounds
Each problem solved teaches something transferable about how to approach the next one.

Curiosity and Problem Solving as a Dynamic Capability

Too much curiosity without structure becomes intellectual wandering: endlessly interesting, rarely impactful. Too much structure without curiosity becomes rigidity: precise and efficient at solving yesterday's problems, blind to tomorrow's. Neither extreme serves well in a rapidly changing environment.

What makes this a powerful identity is the productive tension between the two dimensions. In the organizational strategy literature, the ability to integrate and reconfigure complementary competencies in response to a changing environment is described as a dynamic capability. The Curious Problem Solver identity is dynamic in this sense: not curiosity maximized, not problem solving maximized, but the practiced capacity to draw on both in proportion to what the situation demands. That balance, held consistently, is what separates adaptive thinkers from merely skilled ones.

Section 03
The Identity in Practice

The Integration: What It Means to Be a Curious Problem Solver

When this identity is internalized, these orientations become automatic. They don't follow a strict sequence; the order shifts with the situation. But each surfaces naturally when facing uncertainty or ambiguity:

1
Curiosity First
"What is actually happening? What am I missing? What biases am I bringing to this?"
2
Problem Definition
"What is the core problem? How would I know if it were solved?"
3
Disciplined Exploration
"What solutions are grounded in this problem? What does the evidence suggest?"
4
Iteration as Normal
"Did this work? What did we learn? What do we do differently?"
This identity is not situational. It does not switch off under pressure. It gets stronger through use, both through success, which confirms the identity, and through challenge, which requires defending and deepening it.

A Framework for Critical Thinkers

The Curious Problem Solver identity is, at its core, a critical thinking identity. Critical thinking requires the same two dimensions: openness to what is actually true (curiosity) and precision about what the actual question is (problem definition). Most critical thinking models describe skills to apply. This framework describes an identity to inhabit.

When critical thinking becomes identity, the sequence changes. The curious problem solver asks "What do I actually know here?" before "What should I do?" That ordering distinguishes critical from reactive thinking. It is not a technique retrieved under pressure. It is the default.

How This Relates to Other Frameworks

This framework is not a renaming of adjacent concepts. Growth mindset, as Carol Dweck describes it, addresses beliefs about capability: whether you believe your abilities can develop. Design thinking provides a structured methodology for human-centered innovation. Lean startup offers a build-measure-learn loop for validating ideas under uncertainty. Each is a valuable tool. The Curious Problem Solver framework operates at a different layer: the identity from which you pick up and use those tools.

A curious problem solver applies growth mindset language not because they remember to, but because their default response to failure is curiosity about what to learn next. They run design thinking sprints not because the process demands it, but because they naturally orient to the problem before reaching for solutions. They iterate not because a methodology requires it, but because they expect discovery and treat each cycle as information. Identity is the substrate on which tools and methods sit. Building the identity makes the tools work better and last longer.

Universality and Well-Being

The framework's power extends beyond technology. A curious problem solver approaches a team conflict, a business pivot, a personal setback, or a new market with the same identity: curiosity about what is actually true, precision about what the actual problem is, and disciplined iteration toward a grounded solution.

This universality is intentional and practical. Research on curiosity, growth mindset, and process-based identity consistently connects these orientations to lower anxiety, higher engagement, greater resilience, and sustained well-being. Building a curious problem solver identity is not just an organizational change strategy. It is an investment in sustained performance and well-being.
